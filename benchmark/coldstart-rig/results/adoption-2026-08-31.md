# Adoption proof: upgrading a restored copy of the live deployment

Sub-issue [#1844](https://github.com/NSTA1/Orleans.Lattice/issues/1844) of epic
[#1830](https://github.com/NSTA1/Orleans.Lattice/issues/1830). Every figure here
was taken on the isolated rig in this directory, against a restored **copy** of
the live RepoContext deployment volume. The live deployment was never bound, and
the live image tag never moved.

> **Status: IN PROGRESS.** The baseline, the post-fix cohort and four structural
> findings are settled and reproduced. The long-run index build and the steady
> state after it are still measuring. This file is committed early on purpose, so
> partial honest results are reviewable rather than sitting uncommitted in a
> worktree.

## Headline, stated plainly

The epic **as integrated did not deliver** on the real deployment volume, for a
specific and now-fixed reason. Two of its five structural claims also do not hold
there. Everything below is reproduced from durable state or from a committed
cohort; nothing is inferred.

| # | Finding | Status |
|---|---------|--------|
| **F-A** | The vector trees are **not over-split**. Every tree in the deployment - including all three vector trees - sits at exactly **64 physical shards**, its base. The "1,110 / 1,073 / 454 against 64" figure the epic is built on counts **leaf grains**, not physical shards. | Reproduced on the pristine pre-epic master and on the upgraded volume. |
| **F-B** | Consequently, shard consolidation (S5) and the healing orchestrator (S11) have **nothing to fold**. All 11 healing orchestrators are armed, sweeping, and persisting `NotOverSplit`, which is the **correct** decision. Zero consolidations admitted. | Reproduced from persisted `ShardHealingOrchestratorState`. |
| **F-C** | The **ANN index build never completed**. It aborted every attempt with `EnumerationAbortedException` out of `RepoContextVectorSource.CountAsync`, retried, and aborted again, forever. No index was ever persisted, so query cost stayed proportional to corpus size. Retrieval stayed correct throughout, which is what made it silent. | Reproduced on every build attempt; **fixed**, with failed-first tests. |
| **F-D** | Even with F-C fixed, the index build is a **long incremental background process** that does not finish inside a restart cycle. Across a nine-scenario cohort it reloaded persisted state on **1 activation of 9**; the other eight opened `NotStarted` holding 0 vectors. | Reproduced from the cohort's own container logs. |

F-C is the defect that decides the epic; it is fixed here. F-D is why the fix
alone does not immediately move the cold-start headline, and it is a property of
the design rather than a defect.

## F-A: the trees were never over-split

The epic's evidence table reports leaf counts:

| Tree | Leaves | Physical shards |
|---|---|---|
| `repo-context-vector-payload` | 1,110 | **64** |
| `repo-context-vector-metadata` | 1,073 | **64** |
| `repo-context-vector-membership` | 454 | **64** |
| every other tree | 64 | **64** |

The right-hand column is new here, and it is the one adaptive split and
consolidation actually operate on. A **shard** is what split creates and what
consolidation folds; a **leaf** is a B+ tree node *inside* a shard. Counting
1,110 leaves and comparing them against another tree's 64 compares leaves with
leaves, but the mechanism it motivated - consolidation - compares shards with
the registry-pinned base, and there the answer is 64 against 64 on every tree.

This is measured, not inferred, and it holds on the **pristine pre-epic master**
as well as on the upgraded volume, so it is a property of the deployment rather
than of anything the epic did.

The vector trees do carry roughly 17 leaves per shard where every other tree
carries one. That is a real difference, but it is the shape of a B+ tree holding
35,000 vectors at about 26 KB of persisted snapshot each, not the signature of a
runaway splitter: 1,110 payload leaves at a 583 KB mean is 631 MB of data, and
folding shards would not make it smaller.

**Consequence for the epic.** Finding F3 ("adaptive split is actively harmful;
it multiplied vector-tree activations by about 17x") and finding F4 ("an
over-split tree cannot be repaired, so consolidation is needed") are not
supported on this volume. S5 and S11 are correct, tested, and armed - they
simply have nothing to do here. The activation cost the epic measured is real,
but its cause is leaf granularity and snapshot size, which are S3's and S9's
territory, not the shard count.

## F-B: healing is armed, sweeping, and correctly idle

Read offline from the working volume with the stack down
(`sql/healing-bootstrap.sql`, `sql/healing-decisions.sql`):

- `grain|shard-healing|11` and `reminder|shard-healing|11` - eleven orchestrators
  exist and each has its keepalive reminder registered, so healing bootstrapped
  and survives reactivation exactly as S11 designed.
- Every one of the eleven persists `LastDecision = 4` (`NotOverSplit`).
- Zero `Admitted consolidation` and zero `folding` lines in 100 minutes of
  container log.

Given F-A, `NotOverSplit` is the right answer, and the "refuse to fold on a
guess" behaviour S11 built is doing exactly what it should. This is a
**non-defect**, recorded so nobody reads the absence of folding as a bug.

One genuine sharp edge found while testing this: healing bootstraps lazily from
`LatticeGrain.EnsureMonitorAsync`, which is reached **only from write paths**
(`SetAsync`, `SetIfVersionAsync`, `SetManyWherePredicateAsync` and friends). A
tree that is only ever read never arms its orchestrator. It did not bite here,
because the restored volume resumes an interrupted ingest on boot and that
writes to every tree - but a fully converged, read-only deployment would not
arm healing on the trees that had gone quiet, which are precisely the trees a
bulk ingest would have damaged.

## F-C: the ANN index build aborts, every time

This is the finding that decides the epic.

```
warn: Orleans.Lattice.Api.Mcp.RepoContext.RepoContextAnnIndexRegistry[0]
      Repository-context approximate index build did not complete; semantic
      retrieval continues through the exact path and the build will be retried
      on the next query.
      Orleans.Runtime.EnumerationAbortedException: Enumeration aborted: the
      remote target does not have a record of this enumerator. This likely
      indicates that the remote grain was deactivated since enumeration begun or
      that the enumerator was idle for longer than the expiration period.
         at Orleans.Runtime.AsyncEnumeratorProxy`1.MoveNextAsync()
         at Orleans.Lattice.Api.Mcp.RepoContext.RepoContextVectorSource.CountAsync(
            CancellationToken cancellationToken)
            in src/lattice.api.mcp.repocontext/Retrieval/RepoContextVectorSource.cs:line 160
```

The index opened in phase `NotStarted` holding 0 vectors
(`restored from durable state: False`), started its build, aborted, and repeated
that on every subsequent query for the whole observation window. The vector
plane never reached a serving state.

The mechanism is specific. `RepoContextVectorSource` has two walks over the same
prefix and they are not built the same way:

- `EnumerateAsync` - the streaming half that feeds the build - walks in **pages**,
  through `RepoContextPortability.EnumerateAsync` with an explicit continuation
  token. A page is a request/response; there is nothing to expire between pages.
- `CountAsync` - which only sizes the index's initial reservation and reports
  progress - walks through `tree.KeysAsync(...)`, an **Orleans async
  enumerator**. That enumerator is server-side state with an expiry, and walking
  the entire `repo/{repoId}/vec/` prefix activates every leaf of the
  vector-metadata tree, which on this corpus takes long enough that the
  enumerator is gone before the walk finishes.

So the build is killed by the one call in it that is explicitly documented as
not mattering for correctness: *"the figure is an upper bound rather than an
exact count ... it sizes the index's initial reservation and reports progress,
and nothing depends on it for correctness."* A best-effort progress hint takes
down the whole build.

Note also that this is exactly the failure shape S12 predicted for a different
cause (the missing host access grant): *"the symptom would be 'cold start never
improved' with no error - the exact shape of #1819."* The fault-tolerant
retrieval path is what makes it silent. The grant itself is present and correct
in the integration head; this is a second, independent way to reach the same
symptom.

### Effect on the drop-in measurement

The literal in-place upgrade - same working volume, new image, no volume reset,
no re-index, no configuration change - produced, before the fix:

| | Pre-epic | Post-epic, first boot after upgrade |
|---|---|---|
| `/health/live` | 3.7 - 8.1 s | 4.5 s |
| `/health/ready` | 3.7 - 12.1 s | **148.7 s** |
| First semantic answer | 64.1 - 126.5 s | 150.0 s |
| `retrievalPath` | *(absent)* | `semantic.approximate` |

The readiness figure is **not** a regression and must not be read as one.
`/health/ready` used to be the lifecycle check alone; it is now the conjunction
of the lifecycle check and vector-plane retrieval readiness, so the box now
refuses to claim readiness until it can actually serve a semantic query. That is
the honesty improvement S7 and S12 shipped. It does mean any cross-image
comparison must stop gating its first query on readiness, which is what the new
`-QueryFromLive` cohort option exists for.

A 100-minute observation of that unfixed box, sampling every 30 seconds, is the
clearest picture of what F-C costs: **every** sample answered `semantic` /
`semantic.approximate` and `/health/ready` returned 200 throughout, so the box
looked perfectly healthy - while warm query latency sat between **12 and 47
seconds**, because every query was a full exact scan and no index existed to
serve it.

### The limitation of `retrievalPath` this exposes

Throughout those 100 minutes the box reported `retrievalPath:
semantic.approximate` while holding **no index at all**. That is *correct* under
S12's contract - the declaration is per index rather than per query, and is
documented as a floor on the guarantee, never an overstatement - but the
consequence is worth stating plainly for a future reader:

> `retrievalPath` cannot detect this failure mode. A box that never builds an
> index reports exactly the same value as one whose index is healthy. The
> observable that *does* separate them is **warm query latency** (tens of
> seconds against a few hundred milliseconds), and, in the log, the
> `approximate index build did not complete` warning.

### After the fix

Same volume, same host, unfixed against fixed, on the boot immediately after the
upgrade:

| | Unfixed | Fixed |
|---|---|---|
| Warm query p50 | 12,000 - 47,000 ms | **252 - 322 ms** |
| First semantic answer | 150.0 s | **53.6 s** |
| Index state on open | `NotStarted`, 0 vectors, `restored: False` | `Ready`, `restored from durable state: True` |

That was the first time in the whole run that a persisted index was reloaded
rather than rebuilt.

## The full before-and-after cohort

`cohort-pre-epic.json` against `cohort-post-epic.json`. Three runs each, three
scenarios each, same host, same restored master, same query.

**Comparability.** The pre-epic cohort released its first query when
`/health/ready` returned 200 (3.7 - 12.1 s, median 4.1 s); the post-epic cohort
released it when `/health/live` did (4.3 - 5.3 s, median 4.5 s), because
readiness changed meaning across the epic. The two gates therefore released
within about a second of each other in the typical case, and the two pre-epic
outliers released *later*, which if anything favours the post-epic side. The
first-query comparison is sound. The warm-sample comparison is weaker, because
warm samples start after the first answer and the two sides reached that at
different absolute times.

### Time to first semantic query - NO ATTRIBUTABLE CHANGE

| Scenario | Pre-epic mean | Post-epic mean | Delta | Pre spread | Post spread | Attributable? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| `first-boot` | 87.7 s | 100.6 s | +14.7 % | 71.2 % | 68.9 % | **No** |
| `graceful-restart` | 42.4 s | 57.4 s | +35.4 % | 67.7 % | 42.9 % | **No** |
| `sigkill-restart` | 37.2 s | 47.2 s | +26.7 % | 99.2 % | 26.4 % | **No** |

Every sample answered `semantic` on both sides (9/9 and 9/9), so nothing
degraded to keyword permanently. One post-epic first-boot answered `keyword`
first at 62.7 s and `semantic` at 74.2 s.

**Read this honestly.** Not one of these deltas exceeds its own run-to-run
spread, so under D10 **none of them may be attributed**, in either direction.
The correct statement is that time to first semantic query is **unchanged within
a wide noise floor**, and that the point estimates trend *worse* rather than
better. The epic's headline acceptance criterion - "materially reduced" - is
**not met** by this measurement.

The noise floor is the honest limit here, not a presentational choice. The
samples are bimodal (roughly 24-34 s against 60-129 s) and a single slow run
moves a three-sample mean by tens of percent. A tighter figure needs either many
more runs or a dedicated quiet machine, and it must not be bought by stopping
other people's containers (see the rig README).

### Warm query latency after a first boot - A REAL, ATTRIBUTABLE IMPROVEMENT

| Scenario | Pre-epic p50 samples | Post-epic p50 samples | Ranges overlap? |
|---|---|---|---|
| `first-boot` | 15,959 / 5,307 / 5,368 ms | **210 / 298 / 1,987 ms** | **No** |
| `graceful-restart` | 206 / 179 / 397 ms | 1,106 / 380 / 333 ms | Yes |
| `sigkill-restart` | 180 / 790 / 181 ms | 317 / 874 / 736 ms | Yes |

On `first-boot` the two ranges are **completely disjoint** - the slowest
post-epic sample (1,987 ms) is faster than the fastest pre-epic one (5,307 ms) -
so this one *is* attributable despite the noisy host, and it is roughly an order
of magnitude. Every warm sample on both sides answered `semantic`, so this is
real semantic-path latency and not a keyword fallback.

On the two restart scenarios the ranges overlap and nothing may be attributed.

So the shape of the improvement is: a freshly-restored box becomes usable much
sooner **after** its first answer, while the time **to** that first answer is
unchanged.

## F-D: the index build does not finish inside a restart cycle

With F-C fixed, no build failed: `did not complete` appears **zero** times
across all nine scenario logs, against four in a single activation before the
fix. But the index only *reloaded* persisted state on **one activation out of
nine**:

| Scenario log | Index state on open |
|---|---|
| `first-boot` run 1 / 2 / 3 | `NotStarted`, 0 vectors, `restored: False` |
| `graceful-restart` run 1 / 2 / 3 | `NotStarted`, 0 vectors, `restored: False` |
| `sigkill-restart` run 1 | `Ingesting`, **4,096 vectors**, `restored: True` |
| `sigkill-restart` run 2 / 3 | `NotStarted`, 0 vectors, `restored: False` |

The one restored activation had 4,096 vectors of roughly 35,800 - about 11 % -
after two prior scenarios had run on that volume. The build streams the whole
corpus and persists at flush boundaries, and a cohort scenario lasts two to
three minutes, so a restart almost always arrives before the first flush.

This is not a defect; it is the documented design (S12: "first start on an
existing volume = build; every start after that = reload"). But it means the
epic's benefit is **earned over tens of minutes of uptime**, not at the first
restart, and a measurement built out of short restart cycles - which is exactly
what a cold-start cohort is - cannot show it. That is the single most important
methodological lesson of this run.

## Zero data loss so far

Verified with `verify-corpus.ps1`, which fingerprints the corpus over the box's
own MCP surface and diffs two fingerprints. Baseline captured on the pre-epic box
after it settled:

| Invariant | Pre-epic |
|---|---|
| Files (scan) | 6,824 |
| Files (`list_repos`) | 6,824 |
| Symbols | 67,862 |
| Memory entries | 211 |
| Content spot checks | 4/4 present, digests recorded |
| Known queries | 5/5 answered `semantic` |

The comparator is self-consistent: re-fingerprinting the same box reproduced
every hash exactly.

One instrumentation lesson worth recording. A restored volume **resumes its
interrupted ingest** on boot, so symbol and vector counts climb monotonically
while the box runs - they moved from 35,826 to 42,659 vectors across these runs.
"No data lost" therefore has to be tested as *nothing disappears* (a superset
check that names any missing key), not as *counts are identical*. An earlier
draft that demanded equality would have reported a false regression. Separately,
the first version of the scan reader scraped continuation tokens with a regular
expression and silently mis-paged, which showed up as the symbol count moving
between two passes over an unchanged corpus; it now parses the response as JSON.

## Rig changes made in the course of this work

Two latent defects were found by using the rig rather than by reading it:

1. **`prepare-master.ps1` crashed on a staging manifest written by an older
   revision.** Under `Set-StrictMode -Version Latest`, reading an absent property
   is a terminating error, so a missing `tarballLastWriteTicks` aborted the
   script instead of being treated as a cache miss. Now routed through
   `Test-RigStagingManifestCurrent`, which fails safe to "not current" (and so
   re-extracts, which is always safe) for any manifest it cannot fully read.
2. **`-SkipClone` destroyed the volume it promised to reuse.** The warm-up phase
   cloned the master unconditionally, so a working volume aged or healed over
   hours was overwritten before a single number was taken. The warm-up now clones
   only when the run is going to clone anyway, and refuses `-SkipClone` when no
   working volume exists.

And one gap in the rig's **guidance**, which is worth separating carefully from
its guard, because the guard was never bypassed.

3. **The rig told the operator to do something the rig could not supervise.**
   It measures a CPU-bound window, detects host contention, printed
   "stop them before attributing a delta", and then offered no supported way to
   do that. During this work the operator followed that advice and manually
   stopped the live RepoContext container to quiet the host after a cohort came
   back with a 50-58 % spread. That was an unguarded action taken *outside* the
   rig, and it should not have been taken.

   The isolation guard is **not** implicated, and this was checked rather than
   assumed: `Invoke-RigCompose` builds every invocation as
   `docker compose -p <project> -f <rig compose file>` and runs
   `Assert-RigIsolation` first, there is no unscoped compose path anywhere, there
   is no prune anywhere, and `rig.ps1 clean` routes every removal through
   `Test-RigVolumeName`. The rig did exactly what D11 requires.

   The lesson is that **the rig's isolation governs the rig, not its operator**,
   and that a rig which creates pressure to quiet a host should not also leave
   the operator to improvise how. The advice is now inverted: a contended cohort
   is **recorded** as contended (`hostContext.foreignContainerNames` already
   carries the evidence) and its spread is reported as the host's floor. No
   "quiesce" verb was added, deliberately: a verb that stops other people's
   containers should not exist, because its whitelist becomes the thing someone
   edits under pressure.
